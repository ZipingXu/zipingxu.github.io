Reading Group

Frontier of AI & ML

This reading group focuses on the principles and methods behind modern AI systems. This semester has four themes: uncertainty quantification for generative models, inference from synthetic and model-generated data, game theory and human-AI interaction, and interpretable machine learning.

Topic 1

Uncertainty Quantification for Generative AI

Generative models are fluent whether or not they are correct, so the question is not only what they output but how much to believe it. We read the methods that turn a model's own signals into uncertainty quantification with mathematical guarantees: calibration, semantic entropy, conformal coverage, abstention.

The model here is the object of study. Topic 2 turns it around and asks what happens when the model becomes the measuring instrument.

Topic 2

Inference from Synthetic & Model-Generated Data

The same models are increasingly the measuring instrument. When an LLM judge or a synthetic annotation supplies the labels, the evaluator's error contaminates every number computed from it — and the contamination does not average away.

We ask what valid inference looks like when the data is itself model output: prediction-powered inference and its efficiency theory, then distribution-free wrappers that borrow strength from synthetic data while keeping finite-sample coverage, risk, and FDR guarantees intact.

Topic 3

Game Theory & Human-AI Interaction

The usual assumption: sequential decision making writes the person down as the environment — a fixed, if noisy, map from action to response. It breaks where it matters most, because people learn, they satisfice, and they respond to the pattern of what an algorithm does rather than to its latest message.

The reframe: a game between two learners, each partially observing the other, where what we want is a good equilibrium rather than an optimal policy. Shared interests are not enough — identical payoffs still settle badly when neither side sees the other's history.

Topic 4

Interpretable Machine Learning for LLMs

If we are going to rely on these systems, we should be able to say what they are doing and why. I am interested in the science behind interpretability claims: mechanistic circuits and the causal interventions that test them, sparse dictionary learning together with the critiques that ask whether its features are real, concept-based steering, and the faithfulness of chain-of-thought as an account of a model's actual computation.

The methodological spine is evaluation — probing, sanity checks, and control experiments that distinguish an explanation that is genuine from one that is merely plausible. We close the semester here because it asks what all the earlier signals and behaviors were really evidence of.
